Gen Z Earns in Rupees and Spends in Dollars

Aspirational goods are priced globally. Incomes are not. The gap is not a lifestyle choice — it is a tax, and it is measurable.

Aniket Vinchurkar

There is a story India tells about its twenty-somethings that I think is wrong. The story is that Gen Z is financially reckless: that they over-index on brands, under-save, finance sneakers, and confuse consumption with progress. It is a comfortable story, because it locates the problem inside the consumer.

The uncomfortable version is that they are behaving rationally inside a pricing structure nobody designed for them. The goods that signal adulthood have been globalised. The incomes that pay for them have not.

Two prices that stopped moving together

A flagship phone costs roughly the same in Bengaluru as it does in Berlin. So does a streaming subscription, a pair of running shoes from a global brand, a laptop, a long-haul seat, a coffee from a chain with the same logo on both continents. Global supply chains, global brand architecture and global pricing discipline have made the sticker price of an aspirational basket remarkably flat across countries.

Wages did not flatten. A 23-year-old graduate in Bengaluru and a 23-year-old graduate in Berlin walk into the same store, face the same number on the same box, and hand over wildly different fractions of their life to get it.

Economists have a tool for this and then mostly use it on the wrong things. Purchasing power parity gets applied to national aggregates — GDP, poverty lines, the Big Mac — where it flatters India considerably, because the basket is dominated by things India genuinely does produce cheaply: rent, food, domestic labour, local transport. It is almost never applied to the tradable, brand-led, status-carrying basket that actually defines what a young person feels their money is worth.

Apply it there and the flattery reverses.

The only honest unit is time

Currency is a bad unit for comparing what something costs a person. Hours are a good one. Nobody argues about what an hour is.

So take a good, take the local sticker price including local taxes and duties, and divide it by the median post-tax hourly income of a young earner in that city. You get a number in hours: the real price. Do the same in the United States. Divide one by the other.

The ratio. Hours of median local income to buy the good, divided by hours of median US income to buy the same good. A ratio of 1 means the good costs a young Indian and a young American the same slice of their working life. A ratio of 6 means it costs six times as much.

Almost nothing in the aspirational basket comes in near 1. Devices, imported apparel, international travel, most software priced in dollars, and anything carrying customs duty land far above it. The ratio is worst precisely where the social signal is strongest.

Meanwhile the non-tradable basket — rent outside the metros, food, domestic help, a local haircut, a train ticket — comes in well below 1. This is the part PPP-adjusted GDP sees. It is also the part that carries almost no aspirational weight.

That split is the whole essay. Indian Gen Z lives cheaply in the categories nobody posts about and expensively in the categories that constitute identity.

What the ratio explains

Once you hold the number, a lot of supposedly irrational Indian consumer behaviour resolves into ordinary optimisation.

Barbell spending. Young Indians buy one flagship item and go aggressively downmarket on everything else. This looks inconsistent. It is not. When the ratio is punishing, you cannot afford breadth, so you buy the single item that carries the most signal per rupee and economise ruthlessly elsewhere.

Credit on consumption. A country lectured about financing phones is a country where the phone costs a multiple of a month's income. EMI is not indulgence; it is what happens when a durable good's price outruns the monthly cash cycle. The demand for consumer credit here is a symptom of the ratio, not a moral failure.

Dupes, refurbished and resale. Enormous markets in India, embarrassing ones in the West. They are the market manufacturing its own regional pricing because the brands refused to.

Shared subscriptions. Password-sharing is treated as leakage to be engineered away. It is more usefully read as price discovery: users telling you, through behaviour, what the ratio-adjusted price should have been.

Why this matters if you are building or funding

Most consumer models imported into India assume the Western ratio and then act shocked when conversion breaks. The pattern is consistent: the product is right, the price is nominally reasonable, and the funnel dies anyway. It dies because the real price — in hours — was three to eight times what the same product costs its home market, and no amount of funnel optimisation moves a number that large.

The businesses that work in India are the ones that compress the ratio rather than fight it. Rental and access instead of ownership. Resale and refurbished supply. Embedded credit that reshapes a lumpy price into a cash-flow-shaped one. Genuine regional pricing for software. Local manufacturing that removes the duty layer. Each of these is the same move: take a good with a ratio of six and get it closer to one.

That is the actual India consumer opportunity. Not persuading young people to want things — they already want them, visibly and loudly. It is making the wanting affordable in hours.

The caveats I would raise myself

Medians hide a lot. Indian income distribution is wide enough that a median urban graduate wage describes a fairly narrow band of the people actually buying flagship goods; the top decile faces a much gentler ratio. Household structure matters too — young Indians far more often live with family, which suppresses the housing cost that dominates a Western peer's budget and frees cash for the aspirational basket. Both push the lived experience below what the raw ratio implies.

Neither undoes the argument. They explain how the spending is possible. They do not make the good any cheaper.
