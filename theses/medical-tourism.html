Investment thesis

The Foreign Patient Needs a Brand

India treats the world cheaply and anonymously. The margin is in being the name a patient abroad already trusts.

Aniket Vinchurkar

The claim

India is already one of the largest destinations on earth for cross-border care. It has almost no consumer brands that a foreign patient can name before they get sick. Those two facts sitting next to each other is the opportunity.

Today a patient in Dhaka, Lagos, Tashkent or Muscat does not choose an Indian hospital. They choose an agent. The agent chooses the hospital, takes a commission that can reach a quarter of the bill, and owns the only relationship that matters. The hospital — which supplied the surgeon, the theatre, the outcome and the risk — is interchangeable and knows it.

That is a market with a missing brand layer. Whoever builds it captures the commission, the repeat, and the pricing power all at once.

Why the demand is durable

Indian tertiary care is priced at a fraction of US and Western European equivalents for cardiac surgery, orthopaedics, oncology, transplants and fertility — and the clinical outcomes at the top accredited centres are competitive with anywhere. That gap is structural. It comes from doctor salaries, land, and input costs, and it is not closing.

The inbound flows are not primarily rich Westerners chasing a discount. They are patients from neighbouring South Asia, Africa, Central Asia and the Gulf for whom the alternative is not cheaper care at home — it is no care at home, because the procedure is not available at all. That demand is inelastic, recurring, and largely insulated from the currency and geopolitical swings that get cited as risks.

Policy is pushing the same direction, with dedicated medical visa categories and an explicit national interest in growing medical value travel. Government support is not a thesis on its own, but it removes friction that used to be a real constraint.

What is actually broken

Everything except the medicine.

  • Discovery. A patient's search starts on WhatsApp and ends with whoever answered. There is no trusted, searchable, outcome-transparent way to choose.
  • Price. Quotes are opaque and negotiated per patient. A family liquidating assets to fund surgery abroad cannot get a reliable all-in number before they travel, which is the single most common reason they do not travel.
  • The non-clinical journey. Visa paperwork, airport pickup, accommodation for attendants, language interpretation, food, currency, a SIM card. All of it is improvised. All of it is what the patient's family remembers and repeats.
  • Follow-up. The patient flies home and disappears. Post-operative care happens over unstructured phone calls with a surgeon in another country, or not at all. This is a clinical risk and a wasted commercial asset simultaneously.

Every one of these is a service design problem, not a medical one. Which is precisely why hospitals — organised around clinicians and beds — are bad at solving them.

The model I would want to back

Not a marketplace. Marketplaces in this category become higher-tech agents: they still monetise a one-time referral, and they still do not own the outcome.

A brand. Specifically, a vertically integrated care brand that owns the patient from first search to post-operative follow-up, and that treats Indian hospital capacity as a supply input rather than as the product.

  • Origin-market presence. Physical or clinical presence in two or three source geographies — a diagnostic tie-up, a resident coordinator, a local-language front door. Own the patient before the agent finds them. This is the whole thesis and it is where the capital should go.
  • A fixed, published, all-in price. Procedure, stay, attendant accommodation, transfers, interpretation, a defined complications policy. Bundled pricing is normal in transplant and cardiac care and unheard of for the inbound patient. Publishing it is a brand act, not a pricing act.
  • Asset-light clinical supply first. Contracted capacity and dedicated floors inside existing accredited hospitals. Build or buy beds only once a specific corridor has proven volume — the failure mode here is buying a hospital and then going looking for patients.
  • Structured follow-up in the home country. Teleconsults on a schedule, local partner clinics for physical checks, records that travel with the patient. This converts a one-time procedure into a relationship, and it is the difference between a referral business and a brand.

Narrow to begin with. One or two corridors, two or three procedure lines with high ticket size and clear clinical protocols. Cardiac, orthopaedics, oncology and fertility are the natural starting set.

What kills it

  • Acquisition cost. This is the real risk. If a patient cannot be acquired in the origin market for meaningfully less than the agent commission being displaced, the model is just an expensive agent. Everything depends on that number and I would underwrite it before anything else.
  • Hospital partners defecting. Once a corridor is proven, the partner hospital can go direct. Contracts help; the defence is owning the origin-market relationship and the brand the patient actually recalls.
  • A single bad outcome. Cross-border care has genuine clinical and medico-legal exposure, and a brand concentrates reputational risk that the fragmented agent market currently disperses. This needs to be designed for on day one, not retrofitted.
  • Corridor concentration. Source markets can close on a visa decision or a currency collapse. Two corridors is fragile. Four is a business.
  • Domestic crowd-out. Indian hospitals fill beds with domestic patients too. Guaranteed capacity has to survive a demand surge at home, or the promise to the foreign patient breaks exactly when it matters.

What would change my mind

I would want repeat and referral rate inside the source community — a treated patient who sends two more is the only evidence that a brand exists rather than a funnel. I would want the fully-loaded acquisition cost per patient trending down over consecutive cohorts. And I would want at least one corridor where the company, not the agent, is the first name a patient hears.

Without those, this is an agency with better collateral, and agencies do not earn brand multiples.
