How this place is made

A personal website is one of the last things on the internet you can build entirely to your own taste. This is mine.

Most of the web now arrives through a feed that someone else is tuning. This page is the opposite arrangement: nothing here is ranked, recommended, measured or optimised. It does not know you came and will not notice when you go.

The sky

The colours are not a theme. The page is lit by the actual sky over Mumbai. A few lines of arithmetic work out today’s sunrise and sunset from the date, and the page moves through four states: dawn, day, dusk and night. The small arc in the top corner shows where the sun, or the moon, is right now. Visit at eleven at night my time and the lights are low and there are stars; visit at seven in the morning and it is peach.

If you would rather not read in my weather, the button next to the clock overrides it.

The line at the top

Every line you can type on the front page is an argument from something I wrote. It is the fastest way I know to get someone to actually read a sentence. Nothing you type is sent anywhere, and your best score is kept in your own browser, not mine.

What it is built from

  • No framework and no build step. One stylesheet and three small scripts. View source; it was written to be read.
  • Nothing loads from anywhere else. No analytics, no cookies, no embeds. The fonts are served from this domain too.
  • It works with JavaScript off. You lose the sky and the typing; you keep every word.
  • Type. Fraunces, by Undercase Type, for everything you read, and JetBrains Mono for the small print. Both are open source.
  • A feed you control. There is RSS, which is the one kind of feed I approve of.

Borrowed from

Nothing here is original. These are the places I took from, and what I took.

  • Paul Graham, for the idea that a page of plain links to things you wrote is enough.
  • Low-tech Magazine, whose solar-powered site shows its own weather. A website can admit it exists somewhere.
  • Gwern, for taking the colophon seriously.
  • Robin Sloan, for the phrase and the permission: software can be a home-cooked meal.
  • Patrick Collison, for plain lists over polish.
  • Derek Sivers, for writing to visitors like people.
  • Maggie Appleton, for the garden rather than the stream.
  • Devine Lu Linvega, for proof that a hand-made site can be a life’s work.
  • Craig Mod and Frank Chimero, for what type alone can carry.
  • Monkeytype, for making typing feel like something.
  • Every table of contents in every old book, for the dotted leaders.

That’s the end of the page. The internet rarely lets you reach one.

No cookies, no analytics, no pop-ups, nothing recommended. Every file here loads from this domain and nowhere else.
